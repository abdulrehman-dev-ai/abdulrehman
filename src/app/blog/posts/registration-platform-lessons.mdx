A registration website looks simple until it needs to become a business system.

Behind the public form there are applications to review, supporting documents to protect, cases to track, and customers to update. If approved dealers can submit applications too, the distinction between a public customer and a dealer becomes an architectural decision, not merely a different button in the navigation.

During the Ocean Yacht Registration engagement, those differences informed how I approached the application.

## Give each role a clearly defined journey

A direct customer needs a straightforward registration process. A dealer needs an authenticated space with access to their own submissions. An administrator needs visibility and workflow controls without exposing unnecessary private information.

Those roles may share fields, but they should not share permissions by accident.

Some principles that shaped the implementation:

- Public registration and dealer-originated submissions have distinct entry points.
- Dealer attribution is recorded and validated by the backend.
- Customer records and supporting files are scoped by their intended permissions.
- Administrative case tracking provides a view of progress without treating every user as an administrator.

An identifier can help distinguish where an application originated. It is not a substitute for authorization.

## Keep business rules on the server

A browser can show a quote, but the server should validate pricing and save the relevant terms with the application.

Why? Because the user interface is not a trusted authority. Values submitted from a form can be modified, and a price may change between the first page view and the final application.

For a case-management workflow, the stored price and the relevant service details form part of the operational record. They help explain what was quoted when that particular application was received.

## Treat private documents as private

Identity evidence and registration documents do not belong in the same category as a public marketing image.

A document workflow needs deliberate access boundaries, a durable link to its application, and careful consideration of who can upload, download or review a file. The user interface is just one layer of that protection.

## Make deployment part of the design

In systems that rely on database changes, authentication and third-party services, a successful local build is not enough. Separating staging from production and running smoke checks around the important journeys helps reduce the risk of disrupting a working business process.

## The engineering takeaway

Good client engineering is an exercise in translating real operations into roles, states, rules and safeguards.

I value the visible result, but I spend just as much effort thinking about what happens after someone submits the form: who receives it, who may see it, what changes next, and how the system can be maintained.
